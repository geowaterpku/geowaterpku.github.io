    GeoWater Lab

    Research Overview

    Rivers are not merely conduits that transport water downstream. Their channel geometry, flow pathways, floodplain connectivity, and human modifications form a spatially organized system that controls how water is generated, conveyed, stored, and redistributed across landscapes. GeoWater Lab combines Earth observation, geospatial information, and process-based and data-driven modeling to understand how this organization shapes terrestrial hydrological dynamics and flood–human interactions, moving toward spatially explicit descriptions and predictions of river networks, hydrological pulses, and their changing capacity to convey and accommodate water.

    Three connected questions

    Scientific Questions

    01

    Water-cycle change

    How is the terrestrial water cycle changing under the joint influence of climate and human activities?

    We seek to characterize changes in terrestrial water dynamics from a spatial perspective, with particular attention to how runoff and river flows are redistributed across river networks.

    02

    Configuration × capacity

    How does the spatial configuration of river channels and floodplains regulate hydrological pulses and floods?

    Floods are not determined solely by the magnitude of hydrological forcing. Their propagation, attenuation, storage, and spatial expression depend on the capacity and configuration of river–floodplain systems. We investigate how channel geometry, flow pathways, and floodplain structure shape these processes.

    03

    Rivers × people

    How does the spatial distribution of human development reshape river flows and flood hazards?

    Human activities increasingly reorganize river systems through urbanization, levees, reservoirs, and other interventions. We seek to understand how the spatial configuration of human development interacts with the spatial organization of rivers to alter flow dynamics and flood risk.

    What we study

    Research Programs

    Three connected programs link the physical organization of rivers to hydrological pulses and human development.

    ⌁

    Spatial organization of global rivers

    How are river channels, floodplains, and flow pathways organized across the global river network?

    We develop and integrate large-scale Earth observations and geospatial information to characterize the fine-scale spatial structure of global rivers, including channel geometry, hydraulic properties, floodplain configuration, and river connectivity.

    02
    ∿

    Hydrological pulses and flood dynamics

    How does water move, accumulate, and propagate through spatially heterogeneous river systems?

    We investigate the spatial distribution of runoff, the propagation of flood waves, and the interaction between hydrological forcing and river–floodplain conveyance and storage capacity.

    03
    ⌂

    River–human interactions

    How does human development alter the spatial organization and functioning of river systems?

    We examine how urbanization, levees, reservoirs, and other human interventions reshape river flows and flood dynamics, with the goal of understanding the coupled evolution of rivers and human societies.

    How we work

    A Spatially Explicit Approach to Hydrology

    Addressing these questions requires new ways of representing spatial heterogeneity in hydrological systems.

    01

    Earth observation

    Emerging satellite observations and remote sensing characterize rivers and their dynamics at unprecedented spatial and temporal scales.

    02

    Geospatial information

    Fine-scale river networks, channel geometry, flow pathways, floodplains, and other spatial information represent the physical organization of river systems.

    03

    Process-based and data-driven modeling

    Hydrological and hydraulic models, together with data-driven approaches, connect observations with underlying processes.

    04

    Spatially informed parameterization

    Location-aware and spatially adaptive approaches improve hydrological prediction by explicitly accounting for heterogeneous river systems.

    Enabling Questions

    • How can streamflow be constrained jointly in space and time?
    • How can emerging spatial information be translated into improved river-flow estimates?
    • How can hydrological models adopt location-aware parameterizations that reflect spatially heterogeneous river systems?

    Long-term vision

    Toward a Spatially Explicit Understanding of the Terrestrial Water Cycle

    Ultimately, we aim to develop a more spatially explicit understanding of how water moves through the terrestrial environment—from hydrological forcing and runoff generation, through river-network conveyance and floodplain storage, to downstream propagation and human interaction.
